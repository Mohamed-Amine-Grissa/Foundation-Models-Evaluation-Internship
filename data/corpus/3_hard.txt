Distributed consensus protocols must reconcile the fundamental tension between consistency and availability under conditions of network partition, a constraint formalized by the CAP theorem. In practical deployments, systems such as Raft and Paxos achieve consistency by requiring a quorum of nodes to acknowledge a proposed state transition before it is considered committed, thereby tolerating the failure of a minority of participants without compromising the integrity of the replicated log. However, this quorum-based approach introduces latency proportional to the round-trip time between the leader and the slowest responding follower within the quorum, a cost that becomes increasingly significant in geographically distributed deployments spanning multiple data centers. Engineers attempting to mitigate this latency often introduce eventual consistency models for read operations, accepting a bounded staleness window in exchange for reduced tail latency, though this tradeoff necessitates careful reasoning about application-level invariants that may be violated by stale reads. Furthermore, leader election protocols must account for split-brain scenarios, wherein a network partition causes two disjoint node groups to simultaneously believe they hold quorum, a failure mode typically prevented through the use of monotonically increasing term numbers and strict majority requirements. The interplay between these mechanisms determines the overall fault tolerance characteristics of the system under Byzantine and non-Byzantine failure models alike. Additionally, replicated state machines commonly rely on log compaction techniques, such as periodic snapshotting, to prevent unbounded growth of the underlying operation log, since retaining every historical entry indefinitely would eventually exhaust available storage and degrade recovery time following node restarts. Snapshotting introduces its own coordination challenges, as a node generating a snapshot must ensure that no in-flight operations are omitted or duplicated relative to the log index at which the snapshot was taken, typically enforced through careful sequencing between the snapshot mechanism and the log's commit index. When a lagging follower rejoins the cluster after an extended partition, it may receive a snapshot directly rather than replaying the entire historical log, substantially reducing recovery time at the cost of requiring the follower to reconstruct its state machine from the snapshot rather than incrementally applying each individual operation.
